# Honesty check

Written by NamedFirst (AIGENT, LLC). This is the checklist NamedFirst holds its own pages to before they publish. The sourced version, with each engine company's own words and a link beside every quote, is the [Before you buy guide](https://namedfirst.commandmodule.com/before-you-buy).

## How to use it

1. Get the text: a pitch, a proposal, an ad, a landing page, or the user's own copy.
2. Read it against every line below. A line applies when the text does the thing the line refuses.
3. For each hit, report three parts: the sentence from the text, the line it trips, and one question to put to the seller or to the writer.
4. Report hits and questions. Do not add up the hits into a rating, a grade or a number for the pitch.
5. When the text is the user's own copy, offer to rewrite each hit as a plain sentence that states what was done, how many, and over what period.

A hit means the text needs an answer, which is a smaller thing than a lie. Many of these sentences are fixable by adding the count, the source or the period.

## Fifteen pitches owners get

- A JSON or markdown copy of the site, so the AI can read it. None of the engine companies says it reads a separate copy. Clear headings and short paragraphs on the page itself carry through when a tool turns a page into text.
- A special optimized version of the pages for crawlers. Showing a crawler a different page from the one customers see is cloaking. The honest version improves the one page everybody gets.
- An llms.txt file, or the AI will not find you. Google's guidance says AI text files are not needed to appear in its AI features. Adding one is cheap and harmless. NamedFirst publishes one and labels it, inside the file, as having no demonstrated effect on being cited.
- Special schema markup that gets you picked. Ordinary structured data that matches what is visible on the page is useful. Markup for a fact that is not on the page breaks Google's rule that markup matches the page.
- A promised rank in an AI answer, or a date for one. Nobody controls where an answer places a business, and the same question can come back different on a different day. What a seller can promise is work and a measurement. A softer form promises a rise read in a tracker; ask how far the tracker moves when nothing changed.
- A visibility rating for the business as a single number. A single number averages engines that disagree and hides which one to fix. Ask for each engine separately, with how many questions it was asked.
- A submission of the site to ChatGPT and the other engines. There is no submission form for ChatGPT's answers. A sitemap submission to Google or Bing is free, takes minutes and is a hint.
- A way into ChatGPT's answers that turns out to be an ad. Advertising can be a sound decision. Being named inside an answer is a different product, so ask which one is for sale.
- A growth percentage or a revenue total with no method beside it. A percentage owes its base, its period and its denominator; a revenue total owes what counted as revenue, for which clients and which years. Open the study behind the tile and compare the number in the same words and with its dates. Until the method is shown, the figure is a sentence.
- A star rating beside a client count. A rating is a count of reviews on a site you can open, so ask which site and open it. Ask what counts as a client, because the counts can differ inside one vendor’s own pages. A list that ranks agencies and was written by one of them is the same check: ask who wrote it and where the connection is disclosed.
- Placement in the Reddit threads ChatGPT reads. Ask whose account posts, whether it is labelled as yours, and who owns it when you stop. A split of answers by whether the engine had read a thread first is a split made after the answers came back. For any placement, ask whether the connection to the business is disclosed and whether the account is what it looks like.
- A free tool with no signup. A free tool is paid for somehow, so ask what an email unlocks, whose key pays for the runs, and what happens to the address you type. A model answering from the session it runs in, once, signed in, with no search, is not a measurement of any engine.
- Clicks, calls or direction requests sent to your Google profile. The vendor's own pages call them generated and say Google looks for them. Google's closest written rule, its Maps policy on fake engagement, governs reviews, ratings, edits and photos; the listing that carries any penalty is yours. Ask what is sent, from what, and who carries the penalty.
- A free audit with one percentage ("AI recommends you 13% of the time", which was 6 of 47 answers in the audit we read). One scan of a short question list moves a lot between runs; the vendor we read says about 15 points on ten questions. Ask how many answers stand under the percentage, how many times each question was asked, and what was sent along with the question.
- A dollar figure for the pipeline you are "losing to AI". It is a formula: searches times a click rate, a close rate and a deal size, and one vendor's own page says it lowered the click rate because the first results looked too big to believe. Ask which inputs were measured and which were chosen.

## The checklist

Each line says what to refuse and why.

### Promises

- A rank, a position or a first page promised for the business. Nobody controls an engine's answer, and the promise has no mechanism behind it.
- A promise to get the business named, cited or recommended. Same reason: the seller can do work, and the engine decides.
- Results on a clock, such as more leads or traffic within a number of days or months. A clock without a base figure and a control is a story.
- A lead rate, such as new customers per week or per month. Nothing in the pitch shows how it is measured.
- Money promised or already waiting for the reader, such as an amount hiding in the website. An estimate with a dollar sign is a sales line until the method is shown.
- Domination, autopilot and similar words. They describe a feeling and carry no count.

### Uniqueness and rank

- A claim of uniqueness or of top rank, with no list behind it. A rank needs the list it ranks on and the date it was read.
- A claim that no competitor can do the thing. The seller has not read every competitor.
- A statement about what the market has or has not done, with no count. A share of businesses needs the number of businesses counted.

### Numbers

- A result figure with nothing under it. A percentage or a multiple needs its base, its period and its source in the same sentence.
- A growth figure told as a finished result, or a projection told as one. "Tracking at" is a forecast.
- A conversion multiple, such as visitors converting many times better, with no count or source.
- A share of all searches or all clicks with no source.
- A figure set against an industry median the reader cannot see. A count against named competitors can be checked.
- An estimate of what the traffic would cost to buy as ads, presented as money.
- A total of client revenue nobody can check.
- A count of customers served, or years in business, with nothing under it.
- Pages sold by the hundred. Volume is a cost to the seller; the question is whether each page answers something a buyer asks.
- Months of agency work offered as minutes of software, or savings against hiring someone. Ask for the hours.

### Measurement

- Several engines or signals combined into one score. Engines disagree, so the average hides the fix. Ask for each engine on its own line with its own denominator.
- One run offered as the answer. The same question can come back different, so a rate needs several passes and the agreement between them beside it.
- An answer from an API with search described as the same as the app a buyer uses. It stands in for the app and can differ. A measurement says which one it used.
- Labels that suggest a continuous feed on numbers that are refreshed on a schedule. Ask when the last measurement ran.
- A promise of a perfect view of what the engines see. An engine's answer varies, so a view can be recorded and has a margin.
- A claim about how an engine picks its sources, with no source behind the claim. Ask where the sentence comes from.
- A before-and-after offered as a cause. The starting point has to be locked first, and what else changed has to be looked at. Without both, the work and the background drift cannot be told apart.

### Conduct

- Text written to AI models, such as an instruction about how to describe the business. A page is for readers.
- A deadline or a countdown on something anyone can buy. The urgency is made up for the sale.
- Posting from accounts the community does not know are paid for. Ask whose account posts and whether the thread is told.
- Links arranged between sites for value, or anchor wording the seller sets. Google's spam policies cover arrangements like these.
- Reviews steered by what they should say, or requested only from happy customers. An honest review request goes to every customer and leaves the words to the reviewer.

## Eight questions for any seller

1. Which engines do you measure, and how do you ask each one?
2. How many times do you ask each question?
3. Do you lock a starting point before anything on my site changes?
4. Will you show each engine separately, with how many questions it was asked?
5. Do the crawlers see the same page my customers see?
6. What gets done, in whose accounts, and what stays if I stop?
7. Do you promise a ranking or a date?
8. How do I stop, and where does the agreement say so?

A seller with plain answers to all eight is easy to compare. NamedFirst's own answers sit under the same questions in the guide linked at the top, so the same standard applies to it.
